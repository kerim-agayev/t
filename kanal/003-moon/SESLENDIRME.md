### Tides
- `Tides_0.mp3` [curious] Next, the ocean. The Moon pulls hardest on the side of Earth facing it, and weakest on the far side.
- `Tides_1.mp3`  That difference stretches the oceans into two bulges. As Earth turns, most coasts pass through two high tides a day.
- `Tides_2.mp3`  The Sun does this too. But it is so far away that its tide force is a little less than half of the Moon's.
- `Tides_3.mp3` [serious] Take the Moon away, and a typical tide shrinks to less than half its size. Local coastlines would differ.
- `Tides_4.mp3`  The extra big spring tides and the gentle neap tides vanish. High water would follow the Sun, at about the same hours each day.

### ShoreLife
- `ShoreLife_0.mp3`  That matters at the edge of the sea. The strip that floods and dries each day would likely become narrower.
- `ShoreLife_1.mp3` [warmly] And some creatures keep time with the Moon. In the Gulf of Mexico, many corals spawn together, days after August's full moon.
- `ShoreLife_2.mp3`  Scientists think the lunar cycle, sunlight and water temperature work together as their signal.
- `ShoreLife_3.mp3` [thoughtful] Remove one of those cues, and nobody knows how well that timing would hold.

### EmptySky
- `EmptySky_0.mp3`  Within a month, you notice something else. There are no phases. No crescent, no full moon.
- `EmptySky_1.mp3`  Eclipses of the Moon are over, of course. So are eclipses of the Sun.
- `EmptySky_2.mp3` [excited] We get those thanks to a coincidence. The Sun is about four hundred times wider than the Moon, and about four hundred times farther away.
- `EmptySky_3.mp3` [wistful] So the two look almost the same size in our sky. Without the Moon, that show is over.

### LongDays
- `LongDays_0.mp3`  Now zoom out to centuries. The Moon's tides act like a gentle brake on Earth's spin.
- `LongDays_1.mp3`  Today, each century adds roughly two thousandths of a second to the length of a day.
- `LongDays_2.mp3` [surprised] It sounds like nothing. But one study suggests that one point four billion years ago, a day lasted under nineteen hours.
- `LongDays_3.mp3`  Without the Moon, only the weaker solar tides would keep braking. Our days would lengthen far more slowly.

### Tilt
- `Tilt_0.mp3` [serious] And now, the big question. Earth leans about twenty-three and a half degrees. That lean gives us seasons.
- `Tilt_1.mp3`  The Moon helps hold it steady. Today the tilt only nods by about one point three degrees either way.
- `Tilt_2.mp3`  In nineteen ninety-three, Jacques Laskar and colleagues calculated what happens without that help.
- `Tilt_3.mp3` [dramatic] The result was dramatic. The tilt could turn chaotic, anywhere from nearly zero to about eighty-five degrees.
- `Tilt_4.mp3`  Near that extreme, each pole in turn would point almost straight at the Sun.

### Twist
- `Twist_0.mp3` [curious] That became a famous idea: no Moon, no stable climate. But the story did not end there.
- `Twist_1.mp3`  In twenty twelve, another team simulated moonless Earths over billions of years.
- `Twist_2.mp3` [relieved] Their tilt wandered far more than ours. Yet for hundreds of millions of years, it typically stayed in a band about twenty degrees wide.
- `Twist_3.mp3`  So a moonless Earth would be less steady, but probably not tumbling wildly any time soon.
- `Twist_4.mp3`  What that would mean for climate and life is still being studied.

### Origin
- `Origin_0.mp3`  Which makes you wonder how we got a Moon at all.
- `Origin_1.mp3` [excited] The leading idea: about four and a half billion years ago, a body perhaps the size of Mars hit the young Earth. The debris became the Moon.
- `Origin_2.mp3`  And it is already leaving, very slowly, by about three point eight centimeters a year.
- `Origin_3.mp3` [amused] So slowly that you will never notice. Quite unlike tonight.
