### Hook

- `Hook_1.mp3` [playfully] To see how, hold one finger up at arm's length. Close one eye, then switch.
- `Hook_2.mp3`  Your finger jumps against the wall behind it.

- `Hook_4.mp3` [suspenseful] That small jump is the idea that first measured the distance to a star. But making it work took centuries.

### Trick
- `Trick_0.mp3`  Here is why it works. Your two eyes look from two different places.
- `Trick_1.mp3`  A nearby object seems to move against things far behind it. The nearer it is, the more it moves.
- `Trick_2.mp3`  So if you know how far apart your two viewpoints are, and you measure the size of the jump, geometry gives you the distance.
- `Trick_3.mp3` [warmly] No ruler has to touch anything. Astronomers call this shift parallax.

### Orbit
- `Orbit_0.mp3` [curious] For a star, your eyes are hopelessly close together. We need a wider pair. Luckily, we are riding on one.
- `Orbit_1.mp3`  Look at a star today. Then wait six months.
- `Orbit_2.mp3`  Earth has moved to the other side of its orbit, about three hundred million kilometers away.
- `Orbit_3.mp3`  A nearby star should now sit in a slightly different spot against the distant ones.
- `Orbit_4.mp3` [serious] Simple. Except that, for a very long time, nobody saw it move at all.

### Tycho
- `Tycho_0.mp3`  In the late fifteen hundreds, Tycho Brahe was the finest observer alive. He had no telescope, but his instruments were superb.
- `Tycho_1.mp3`  He found no shift. So he faced a choice.
- `Tycho_2.mp3` [thoughtful] Either Earth does not travel around the Sun, or the stars are unbelievably far away, with an enormous emptiness in between.
- `Tycho_3.mp3`  That emptiness seemed absurd to him. It was one of his main objections to a moving Earth.
- `Tycho_4.mp3`  The shift was real. It was just far too small for him. Even his best work was at least twenty times too coarse.

### Hooke
- `Hooke_0.mp3`  Telescopes raised hopes. In sixteen sixty-nine, Robert Hooke built one into his rooms at a London college.
- `Hooke_1.mp3`  It looked straight up, through holes in a floor and the roof. He lay on the floor beneath it and watched a star called Gamma Draconis.
- `Hooke_2.mp3`  It passes almost directly overhead, where the air bends starlight least.
- `Hooke_3.mp3` [excited] In sixteen seventy-four, he announced success. But he had only four observations, from a troublesome instrument.
- `Hooke_4.mp3` [amused] Other astronomers were not convinced. They were right.

### Bradley
- `Bradley_0.mp3` [surprised] In seventeen twenty-five, Samuel Molyneux and James Bradley set out to repeat Hooke's work. And the star moved!
- `Bradley_1.mp3`  Back and forth, in a smooth yearly cycle. But the timing was wrong. It was three months off from what parallax predicts.
- `Bradley_2.mp3` [curious] Bradley found the answer. Imagine walking through falling rain.
- `Bradley_3.mp3`  You tilt your umbrella forward, because your own motion changes where the rain seems to come from.
- `Bradley_4.mp3`  Light does the same to a moving Earth. This is called the aberration of starlight, and it is direct evidence that we really are moving.
- `Bradley_5.mp3`  Parallax was still hiding underneath. Bradley concluded it was probably less than half an arcsecond.
- `Bradley_6.mp3` [awe] That put his star more than four hundred thousand times farther away than the Sun.

### Suspect
- `Suspect_0.mp3`  An arcsecond is one three-thousand-six-hundredth of a degree. To catch something that small, you must pick the right star.
- `Suspect_1.mp3` [curious] Which ones are close? Brightness can fool you. But there is a better clue.
- `Suspect_2.mp3`  Watch from a passing train: nearby trees rush by, while far hills barely move.
- `Suspect_3.mp3`  In the sky, a faint double star called Sixty-one Cygni was drifting unusually fast from year to year. That suggested it was near.
- `Suspect_4.mp3`  And from his observatory, the German astronomer Friedrich Bessel could follow it all year. He made it his target.

### Heliometer
- `Heliometer_0.mp3` [impressed] Bessel had a special instrument, a heliometer, the last design of the great lens maker Joseph Fraunhofer.
- `Heliometer_1.mp3`  Its main lens was sliced into two halves. Slide one half, and every star splits into two images.
- `Heliometer_2.mp3`  Bessel slid the glass until the image of his target met a faint neighbor.
- `Heliometer_3.mp3`  A fine screw told him how far the lens had moved, and so, the angle between the stars.
- `Heliometer_4.mp3`  Comparing neighbors was the clever part. Bradley's effect shifts them both almost equally, so it cancels out.
- `Heliometer_5.mp3` [excited] A shift that belongs only to the nearer star remains.
- `Heliometer_6.mp3`  He did this again and again, about a hundred times against each of two faint stars, across a whole year.

### Wave
- `Wave_0.mp3` [serious] Then came the arithmetic, all by hand.
- `Wave_1.mp3`  He had to separate the star's steady drift across the sky from anything that repeated once a year.
- `Wave_2.mp3`  Point by point, a pattern appeared. Forward, then back.
- `Wave_3.mp3` [amazed] A gentle wave, keeping time with Earth's orbit. There it was.
- `Wave_4.mp3`  Astronomers define parallax as half of the full swing, the shift caused by one Earth to Sun distance.
- `Wave_5.mp3`  In eighteen thirty-eight, Bessel announced a value of about zero point three one four arcseconds.
- `Wave_6.mp3` [awe] For the first time, a star had a convincing distance. And the shift that Tycho had looked for was finally there.

### Distance
- `Distance_0.mp3` [playfully] How small is that? Picture a one euro coin about fifteen kilometers away. Its width covers about the angle Bessel measured.
- `Distance_1.mp3`  Now draw the triangle. Its short side is the distance from Earth to the Sun, which astronomers had estimated by watching Venus cross the Sun in the seventeen sixties.
- `Distance_2.mp3`  Its sharp tip is at the star. The rule is beautifully simple. Half the angle means twice the distance.
- `Distance_3.mp3`  Bessel's angle gives about ten light years. Today's value, measured from space, is about eleven point four.
- `Distance_4.mp3` [impressed] With a split lens and patient eyes, he came within about ten percent.
- `Distance_5.mp3`  A later study suggests that temperature changes in the instrument can explain the gap.
